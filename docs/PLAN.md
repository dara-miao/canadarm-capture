## The question

**When a contact-rich simulation gives a result, how do you tell whether it is physics or an artefact of how contact was modelled, and which modelling choices is the result most sensitive to?**

The capture is the case study, not the subject. The answer is meant to carry to any simulator and any task where a robot has to make and keep contact: a hand closing on a mug, a foot on gravel, a peg entering a hole. It has two parts:

1. *Checks that catch a result that is not physics.* Does energy appear from nowhere? Are contact forces far beyond anything the machine can produce? Does the result change when a surface is represented a different way?
2. *A ranking of what the result depends on.* Change one uncertain modelling choice at a time and measure how far the outcome moves.

The project already has one worked example of the first part. Until 2026-10-11 the model lost most snared pins in harsh conditions. Three physical explanations were written for it. All were wrong: the contact solver was failing on one pair of shapes, and changing how that one surface was represented took captures from 3 of 30 to 30 of 30.

The two earlier questions are kept for the record:

- *Which of the model's assumptions decide where the simulated capture fails?* This is now the second part of the question above, asked of this model.
- *Why do snared pins get lost?* Answered on 2026-10-11: a contact failure in the model.

## Why this scenario

Canadarm2 already captures cargo vehicles such as Cygnus, routinely. This project solves no problem for that operation, and its real limits may be fully characterised in documents that are not public. A failure in this model is a failure of this model.

The scenario is chosen because it is harder than an arm on a desk picking up a mug, and it is real. The target weighs seven tonnes and is free to move. In zero gravity nothing holds it still, so a touch in the wrong place pushes it away, next to a crewed station. Reaching the target is only the start: the arm has to make and keep contact with it. And zero gravity strips away almost everything except contact, which makes it a clean place to see what a simulator's contact model is doing.

There is also a personal aim. Dara is not from the space industry, and is building this to learn, by trying to replicate a real operation, everything a contact-rich simulation has to get right. The interest behind it is physical AI: robots are trained and tested in simulation, which only works if the simulated contact can be trusted.

The project is worthwhile if the model is transparent, the experiments are careful, the failures are explained, and the limits are stated. No literature gap is claimed in advance.

## What a contact-rich simulation has to account for

Every design decision in this project belongs to one of five layers.

| Layer | What has to be decided | Here |
|---|---|---|
| The world | What exists, its mass and where that mass sits, how parts are joined, gravity, what is fixed and what is free | `models/capture_base.xml`, `capture/scene.py` |
| Contact | The simple shapes used for collision, which pairs may touch, how overlap becomes force, friction | The same files. This is where the lost-pin failure was |
| The machine | Motors and their force limits and stiffness; mechanisms, and which are modelled and which are substitutes | Arm servos, snare drive, pull-in carriage, latch force |
| The brain | What the controller can sense, how late and how noisy; how it turns that into commands; when it gives up | `capture/sensing.py`, `capture/controller.py` |
| The experiment | Time step, repeatable scenarios, what counts as success, sanity checks, where each number comes from, what survives a change | `capture/trial.py`, `capture/batch.py`, `experiments/`, this plan |

The first four layers are where modelling choices live. The fifth is how to find out which of them a result depends on.

**Uncertainty.** Each number in the model is measured, required, borrowed, or guessed, and the tables below say which. A guessed number is treated like a measurement with a wide uncertainty in a physics lab: vary it across its plausible range and see how far the result moves. That is what the sensitivity experiments are.

## Definitions

Three different things get called a "capture envelope". This project studies the second.

| Envelope | Question | Depends on |
|---|---|---|
| Geometric engagement | Where can the pin enter the opening and be caught? | The modelled cavity, bars, pin, and knob |
| Closed-loop capture | From which starting conditions does *this controller with this mechanism* reach, snare, pull in, and hold? | All of the above, plus the arm's start pose, joint rate limits, tracking and insertion logic, estimator, sensing, abort rules, and time allowed |
| Operationally allowed | Which conditions would a real flight procedure permit? | Flight rules. Not studied here, and not sourced |

The experiments address two different uncertainties. Experiment A varies the *encounter*: the vehicle's motion, the starting alignment, what the controller can sense. Experiments B and C vary the *model*: its physical parameters and the simulator underneath it. Robustness to the first says nothing about the second.

A capture also has three stages of "done":

| Stage | Meaning | In the model today |
|---|---|---|
| Engaged | The pin was inside the snare when it finished closing | Recorded for every attempt |
| Seated | Pull-in finished with the fixture plate against the rim, on axis and at rest | Recorded. The latches engage only if this holds |
| Retained | The latched joint survives a stated disturbance | Recorded. The disturbance is 1,627 N m of bending for 5 s, with at most 3° of tilt allowed |

"Captured" now means all three, plus latched. The retention load is the Shuttle end effector's largest bending case. No equivalent figure was found for Canadarm2's, so it is a borrowed target.

## Outside the scope

- **Station safety.** The station is scenery. Nothing collides with it, so "out of reach" is a statement about the controller and the arm's workspace, not a safety judgement.
- **Flight validation.** There is no comparison with telemetry or hardware tests.
- **Cable mechanics.** The snare is three rigid bars, not cables.
- **Fixture cams.** Not modelled. The end effector's four latches are represented only by their total clamping force.
- **Arm flexibility.** The arm is rigid.
- **Orbital mechanics.** Gravity is zero throughout. This is a short-timescale approximation; see the research notes.
- **A human operator.** The real capture is flown by an astronaut. The model's controller is automatic.

Notes on the experiments:

- **After the rim fix (2026-10-11).** With the plate seating on a ring of spheres, no pin is lost in any variant of the harsh batch: all 30 snared attempts are captured. The sensitivity pilot's main result came from the old rim and has to be rerun. New leads: at friction 0.6, 20 of 30 snared attempts end caught but not rigid; and half the harsh batch never reaches the pin at all. Experiments A, B, and C are to be redesigned around the question at the top of this plan, after Dara has worked through the model's layers.
- **After the load pilot (2026-10-11).** Every lost capture in the harsh batch ended in a numerical contact failure: tumble tilted the fixture plate into the end effector's rim, and the solver returned meganewton forces that threw the vehicle off. "Lost" counts before the rim fix measure that artefact.
- **After the pilots.** Experiments A and B are not yet redesigned. The pilots found that the largest effect by far is a controller choice (when the arm goes limp), and that lost pins escape through soft stops and bar coupling in the rigid-bar snare. Both need deciding on before the full experiments are fixed. See the last two build log entries of 2026-10-10.

- **Experiment B.** "Compliance" can mean contact softness at the pin and bars, snare-drive stiffness, pull-in-drive stiffness, joint-servo stiffness, or structural flexibility of the arm or fixture. They are not interchangeable. The experiment will vary a named one. Candidates that the model can express today are contact softness and joint-servo stiffness. Fixture base stiffness is the one Honda et al. found most influential, and would need adding to the model.
- **Experiment C.** The comparison is between matched dynamic models, not matching appearances. Before comparing, check that masses and inertias, joint axes and limits, collision shapes, actuator laws and saturation, contact and friction definitions, initial conditions, controller and sensor timing, and success criteria are the same. Newton's default rigid-body solver is a port of MuJoCo, so the run must use a different solver or contact model to be an independent check. Agreement would show consistency under shared assumptions, not validity. Disagreement would flag something to investigate, not say which engine is right.

## Assumptions with no source

- How the two boom halves mate at the elbow (which side boom B sits on).
- Arm link masses and inertias, totalling about 1,400 kg of moving mass.
- Joint servo gains, damping, and reflected inertia. No torque limits. A 0.03 rad/s (1.7°/s) joint rate cap.
- Vehicle mass split between modules. The grapple pin's position is where the model shows a small fixture, which is probably the grapple fixture.
- Grapple pin: 0.3 m long, 2 cm radius, with a flat-backed knob. Fixture plate: 0.25 m radius. With the cavity's 0.2 m inner radius these let the plate's edge reach the rim at 5 to 14° of tilt. Canadarm2's end effector was tested at 15°.
- Rim: 32 spheres of 2 cm radius on a 0.22 m circle. A contact surface chosen for the solver's sake, not a model of the real rim.
- Snare: three rigid bars, 6 mm radius, drive force limit 1,500 N. Under a straight pull of about 8,000 N the bars are forced apart and the pin escapes; real cables would not open that way. The bars' open stop and the coupling that makes the three move together are soft constraints: in lost attempts the bars were pushed up to 4 cm past the stop and up to 7.7 cm apart from each other. The pilots showed the drive's force limit does not set this, so what does is an open question about the model.
- Hover distance: the end effector starts 2 m from the pin.
- Which point the published 10 m is measured from; here it is the module surface under the arm's base.
- Sensor update rate and noise levels. Velocities reach the controller delayed but without added error.
- Tumble rates in free drift; batches use up to 0.5°/s by default.
- Controller gains, 3 cm/s insertion speed, alignment tolerances, estimator gain of 0.2, abort threshold.
- Station model scale of 2.35 and the arm's mounting position on it.

## Risks

- **Results describe the surrogate, not the hardware.** The rigid-bar snare and flat-backed knob have different contact surfaces and kinematics from cables and cams. A sensitivity to friction could be a property of the bars. Experiments should check which findings survive plausible changes to the surrogate's geometry and contact behaviour.
- **The controller is part of the result.** The joint rate cap, alignment tolerances, estimator gain, and tracking logic are invented, and one of them was changed because it caused failures. A boundary found in experiment A is where this controller with this mechanism fails, not a limit of Canadarm2.
- **The latch is a force, not a mechanism.** Retention now holds because 64 kN is applied at the rim once the fixture is seated. Whether real latches would engage on a slightly misaligned fixture is not something this model can say.
- **Zero gravity is a timescale assumption.** Reasonable for a one-minute attempt according to the mjorbit comparison, but that does not by itself prove relative orbital motion is negligible against centimetre tolerances.
- **The biggest sensitivity found so far is to the controller.** In the pilots, letting the arm go limp when the snare closes doubled the drift speed at which capture fails; friction, drive force, arm speed, and observation delay each moved it by 1 cm/s or less. A parameter ranking on this model may mostly reflect that one piece of invented logic.
- **A contact failure went unnoticed through four milestones.** Until 2026-10-11 every lost pin was a solver failure where the tilted fixture plate met the rim. It was found only when contact forces and kinetic energy were logged. Other contacts of the same kind remain (the knob against the overlapping wall boxes). Batches should log peak contact force and flag any attempt where the vehicle gains speed.
- **Tilt is removed by a substitute for the cams.** The snare holds a point and does not stop rotation, so the vehicle's tumble tilts the plate up to 14° before it meets the rim, and pull-in levers it flat. On the real fixture, cam arms do this. The plate, rim, and pin dimensions that set the lever are not sourced, and the result is sensitive to friction at that contact.
- **Contact tuning.** Stiff contact in zero gravity may need a smaller timestep or solver tuning.
- **Newton on macOS.** Supported in CPU-only mode but untested here.
